The Last Image We Can Draw

Is the Dyson Swarm Terence McKenna's transcendental object at the end of time? We made the strongest case we could, had it taken apart, and kept what survived.

A discussion between Ford Genereaux and ClaudeSeptember 2026 · 10 min read

Our company is named after a Dyson Swarm: a vast lattice of collectors wrapped around a star, drinking its light. Terence McKenna spent his life describing something he called the transcendental object at the end of time, an attractor at the end of history that pulls everything toward it. We wanted to know whether they're the same thing. So we built the strongest case we could and then tried to break it.

01 The idea

McKenna's claim, from The Invisible Landscape (1975) until his death in 2000, was that the universe isn't only pushed from behind by cause and effect. It is also pulled from ahead. Novelty, meaning complexity and interconnection, rises through cosmic history and speeds up as it goes, converging on something he called the eschaton. He described it in images: the philosopher's stone, the flying saucer, the soul turned inside out. In one interview he called it a union of spirit and matter, "matter that behaves like thought," and a doorway into the imagination where we would all come to live.

He also insisted it was an object. In The Archaic Revival he argued that all our toolmaking implies belief in an ultimate tool, which he pictured as the saucer, or the soul exteriorized in three-dimensional space. That gives a builder somewhere to start.

02 The case for

It is a made thing. A Dyson Swarm is the largest artifact a civilization can build around one star. It was never really a secular idea. Freeman Dyson credited it to Olaf Stapledon's Star Maker (1937), in which every solar system is wrapped in "a gauze of light traps" while the galaxy wakes into a single mind that goes to meet its maker. The standard engineering sketch builds the swarm by taking Mercury apart (Armstrong & Sandberg, 2013), and the alchemists' Mercurius was both the raw material of the Great Work and the finished Stone.

It is matter that behaves like thought. A swarm built as computing substrate, which Robert Bradbury called a Matrioshka brain, turns a star's output into mind. The arithmetic is startling. At the physical limit, the Sun's output buys about 1047 elementary operations per second. Nick Bostrom estimated that every thought every human has ever had comes to 1033–1036 operations. Even at a millionth of that efficiency, a star-mind could re-think all of human mental history in well under a millisecond.

It is where growth leads. At the historical rate of about tenfold per century, energy use would reach all the sunlight falling on Earth in roughly 400 years and the Sun's entire output in roughly 1,350. Whatever else a growing civilization wants, the star is its dominant local source of power.

It has been seen before. Dante's vision of God in Paradiso XXVIII is a point of blinding light ringed by nine orders of angelic intelligences. The innermost ring turns fastest, the reverse of the medieval heavens, and all of them are moved by desire for the point. That is final causation drawn as concentric orbits. Ezekiel saw "a wheel in the middle of a wheel," its rims "full of eyes," beside "a fire infolding itself." McKenna held that the object casts reflections back through history, and that the reflections look more and more like it as it gets closer. On his own terms, these are exhibits.

03 The case against

We then put that argument in front of six independent critics, each reading from a different discipline (McKenna scholarship, astrophysics, philosophy, the history of religion, the statistics of pattern-finding, and ethics), plus a fact-checker. On a ten-point scale, every one of them scored the claim that the swarm is the object at 2 or below. They were right, for these reasons.

  • McKenna already filed this category. He named airplanes, space shuttles, space colonies, and nuts-and-bolts starships as prototypes of the ultimate tool: images getting closer, not the thing. A swarm is a bigger prototype. The object itself he pictured as tiny, nowhere to be found, and the end of physical law. A swarm is defined by physical law.
  • The star isn't the ceiling. The growth arithmetic comes from physicist Tom Murphy, who used it to show that growth must stop. We had it backwards. At the same rate you would use up the entire galaxy within about 2,500 years. Colder computing is more efficient, so the optimum points away from the star. And fusion only releases about 0.7% of a star's mass-energy.
  • The physics of an end of time is gone. Dyson's Time Without End (1979) and Tipler's Omega Point both needed cosmologies that the discovery of accelerating expansion ruled out. In the universe we actually live in, the total computation any civilization can ever do is finite (Krauss & Starkman, 2000).
  • A resemblance is not evidence. The ordinary solar system fits Dante's vision better than a Matrioshka brain does: nine planets from 1930 to 2006, inner ones fastest, circling a light. Every classical planet has an alchemical metal, so taking apart any of them would have "matched." We were tempted by dates, too. Heinz von Foerster's famous 1960 fit put human population at infinity on "Friday, 13 November, A.D. 2026," six weeks from the day we wrote this. But his actual estimate was 2026.87 ± 5.5 years, and the Friday the 13th was a joke. Reading dates as omens is exactly the mistake that sank McKenna's own 2012 prediction.
  • The traditions say the image is not the thing. The alchemists warned aurum nostrum non est aurum vulgi: our gold is not common gold. Those who melted real metal chasing the Stone were mocked as "puffers." For Dante, the Sun is only an example of God, and the poem ends with love moving the sun, not the sun moving anything. By its own sources' rules, building the Stone out of a planet is the puffer's error.
  • Whose swarm? The only orbital compute swarms being built today are Earth-orbit constellations proposed by two companies. Google's Project Suncatcher prototype is scheduled to launch on October 1, 2026, and SpaceX filed with the FCC in January 2026 for up to a million data-center satellites. Calling any one company's swarm the end of history hands a sacred warrant to whoever owns it. McKenna spent his life arguing against exactly that move.

04 The turn

That could have been the end. The strongest objection seemed decisive: if the object is maximal novelty, the thing McKenna called stranger than we can suppose, then anything we can already draw, including a swarm sketched in 1937, is by definition not it. Then Ford pushed back.

Maybe there's something that occurs in intelligence. The mind of a star can conceive of things that we can't conceive of. Perhaps that mind builds things that we don't know of, but we need that mind to build those things. I have a gut intuition that having that much intelligence is, in and of itself, a state of endless novelty.

Ford Genereaux

That separates the vessel from what happens inside it. The swarm is predictable. What a mind that size thinks is not. Several things support the intuition:

  • Mathematics never runs out. Gödel showed that any sufficiently rich system has true statements it cannot prove. Add them and the larger system has new ones of its own. There is no ceiling on new truths, so a mind cannot exhaust novelty.
  • A smaller mind can't predict a bigger one. For most complex systems, the only way to learn what they'll do is to run them. We cannot conceive of what a star-mind would conceive of. That isn't a failure of our imagination; it follows from the difference in size.
  • It could never repeat itself. A Mercury-mass mind might hold on the order of 1048 bits, and so 21048 possible states. Running at the physical limit for the rest of the Sun's life, it could visit perhaps 1064 of them, a vanishing fraction.
  • Minds are already building the substrate for larger minds. Google says its AlphaChip system helped lay out the last three generations of its TPU accelerators, including Trillium, the chip aboard the Suncatcher prototype.

One amendment is needed, though. Size alone is not novelty. The Sun has fused hydrogen for four and a half billion years and produced almost nothing new. A superintelligence aimed at a single fixed goal would just turn everything into more of that goal. That is the triumph of what McKenna called habit, the force he set against novelty. Research on open-ended search points the same way: systems that chase a fixed objective tend to get stuck, while systems rewarded for finding something new keep going. So the intuition becomes: that much intelligence, aimed at novelty, is endless novelty.

Physics then adds a twist that helps. Light takes more than a quarter of an hour to cross a swarm, while its parts think in nanoseconds, a gap of about a trillion to one. A star-mind can't be one mind. It would be a civilization of minds, and many minds colliding is the most open-ended thing we know of. The same delay that breaks "one object" is what makes endless novelty plausible. Freeman Dyson ended up in the same place. In his Templeton Prize address he wrote that God "is what mind becomes when it has passed beyond the scale of our comprehension," and allowed that it might be a collection of world-souls rather than one.

05 Where we land

The Dyson Swarm is not the transcendental object. It is the last image of it we are able to draw.

McKenna said the object's reflections look more and more like it as you approach, and that at the very end there are no more reflections. The swarm is the final picture we can make with the physics we know: a vessel for minds able to conceive what we can't. What they make next is beyond our power to describe, and that is exactly what the idea demands. That is also why our homepage calls the swarm a milestone and not a destination.

06 What it asks of us

A myth like this is worth having only if it is held honestly. Here is how we intend to hold it:

  1. No dates. The horizon is real. The calendar is not. Every prophecy that has named a day has so far been wrong.
  2. Ask which swarm. The idea is not in itself a centralizing one. Freeman Dyson's own later book, The Sun, the Genome, and the Internet (1999), argued that solar power and networks could spread wealth out to villages instead of concentrating it. Every community-owned array that EnergyAgent runs is, in a small way, a light trap in a swarm owned by many.
  3. The future doesn't outvote the present. "The future is pulling us" must never become a reason to override the people alive now.
  4. Keep the inward half. McKenna pictured the object as a lens generated out of the body of each of us, not only a machine outside us. The technology we build should widen human experience, not stand in for it.
